# Survey of Video and Multimodal Generation

## TL;DR
- Foundational video generation moved from early diffusion-based systems to discrete-token and cascade designs: Video Diffusion Models, Make-A-Video, Phenaki, and Imagen Video show the main representational split between continuous diffusion over latent/video space and tokenized generation with temporal attention.[1][2][3][4][5][6][7][7]
- Recent progress is dominated by diffusion-transformer backbones plus stronger conditioning, especially for image-to-video and multi-prompt control: CogVideoX, ConsistI2V, STIV, CausVid, DiTCtrl, DiTraj, and MotionRAG each add a different route to better motion, consistency, or controllability.[8][9][10][11][12][13][14][15][16]
- The topic has expanded beyond pure video synthesis into unified multimodal generation, where video is coupled with audio, text, and reference images/videos; JoVA and Foley-Omni explicitly unify video-audio-text generation or soundtrack synthesis, while LEGO and ReCamMaster show camera- and input-video-conditioned generation.[17][18][19][20]
- Evaluation is shifting from generic quality scores toward benchmarked controllability, dynamics, and safety: WorldScore evaluates world-generation dimensions, while T2VSafetyBench, SAFESORA, and Video-SafetyBench focus on temporal safety risks and human preference alignment.[21][22][23][24][25]
- A recurring open problem is long-horizon degradation: long-rollout video often becomes static or loses quality, so methods now try autoregressive decoding, long-horizon teacher forcing, or retrieval-augmented motion priors to keep dynamics alive.[12][15][21]

## Background
Video generation aims to synthesize temporally coherent frame sequences from text, images, or other visual context, while multimodal generation extends the goal to cross-modal outputs such as video-plus-audio or video conditioned on multiple input modalities.[2][4][26][7][17][19][20] The foundational design choice is whether the model generates continuous latent video directly with diffusion-like denoising or first compresses video into discrete tokens and then generates those tokens with a transformer.[4][6][7] A parallel line of work adapts multimodal language-model ideas to visual inputs and video, showing that interleaved image/video/text context can be handled in a single architecture even when the target task is understanding rather than synthesis.[26]

Early evidence for the field’s core formulations comes from Video Diffusion Models, Make-A-Video, Phenaki, and Imagen Video.[2][4][6][7] Video Diffusion Models establish diffusion as a direct extension of image diffusion to video, Make-A-Video shows that text-to-video is possible without paired text-video data, Phenaki demonstrates variable-length generation with discrete video tokens and causal temporal attention, and Imagen Video scales diffusion with a cascade of base and super-resolution models.[2][4][6][7]

## Continuous diffusion and tokenized video foundations
The earliest retrieved foundations split into two camps.[2][4][6][7] In the diffusion camp, Video Diffusion Models and Imagen Video treat video synthesis as denoising in a latent video space, emphasizing joint image/video training, longer-horizon extension, and progressively higher fidelity through cascaded super-resolution.[2][6][7] In the tokenized camp, Phenaki converts video into discrete tokens and then uses a masked transformer to generate those tokens from text, which makes variable-length video synthesis more natural.[4] Make-A-Video sits between those camps by explicitly borrowing text-to-image advances and extending them to video with spatial-temporal modules and a video decoder pipeline.[2][4]

These foundations also establish the first major tradeoff in the literature: diffusion models are strong on fidelity and controllability but can be slow, while token-based approaches can better support variable length and compositional generation but rely heavily on representation quality.[4][6][7] The retrieved abstracts and summaries do not provide full benchmark tables, but they consistently claim state-of-the-art or strong qualitative/quantitative results, indicating that the foundational race was already about temporal coherence, text alignment, and scalability rather than only single-frame realism.[2][4][6][7]

## Diffusion transformers, control, and efficiency
Recent systems increasingly build on diffusion transformers rather than plain U-Nets or earlier diffusion stacks.[8][16] CogVideoX compresses video with a 3D VAE and uses an expert transformer for text-video fusion, while STIV unifies text-to-video and text-image-to-video with frame replacement and joint image-text conditional classifier-free guidance.[8][16] These systems suggest that the frontier has moved toward architectures that treat text, images, and video as interchangeable conditioning signals rather than separate tasks.[8][16]

A second trend is better control at test time, often without retraining the entire generator.[12][13][14] DiTCtrl manipulates attention to support multi-prompt transitions in long videos, and DiTraj modifies foreground token position embeddings to improve trajectory adherence.[13][14] CausVid takes a different route by converting a bidirectional diffusion transformer into a causal, autoregressive generator and distilling it for much faster streaming generation.[12] Together these sources show an emerging pattern: instead of only scaling model size, recent work tries to add controllability, prompt composition, and real-time speed on top of a pretrained backbone.[12][13][14]

## Image-conditioned, video-conditioned, and retrieval-augmented generation
Image-to-video and video-conditioned generation have become central because many practical use cases need consistency with an existing visual reference.[10][11][15][18] ConsistI2V focuses on first-frame anchoring through spatiotemporal attention and low-frequency noise initialization, explicitly targeting subject, background, and style consistency.[10][11] STIV broadens this idea into a single framework for text and image conditioned generation, with reported benchmark gains on both text-to-video and image-conditioned video tasks.[16] ReCamMaster and LEGO further show that conditioning can come from a full input video: one reconstructs or re-renders scenes with novel camera trajectories, and the other lifts an exocentric video into egocentric video without explicit geometry lifting.[18][17]

Retrieval is another recent addition to conditioning.[15] MotionRAG adds motion retrieval and motion adaptation modules to image-to-video generation, aiming to inject motion priors rather than learn all dynamics from scratch.[15] This suggests a different efficiency-quality tradeoff from pure scaling: retrieve useful dynamics, then adapt them to the new prompt or reference image.[15] In the retrieved evidence, this family is still early and mostly repository-driven, so the strongest claims are about practical improvement and low overhead rather than fully standardized benchmark dominance.[15]

## Multimodal generation, evaluation, safety, and open problems
The most recent evidence shows that video generation is becoming part of broader multimodal generation systems.[17][19][20] JoVA unifies video-audio generation and editing with direct video, audio, and text interaction plus image/video reference conditioning, while Foley-Omni extends soundtrack generation to speech, sound effects, and music inside a shared latent process.[19][20] These systems indicate that “video generation” is increasingly incomplete without synchronized audio and editable multimodal context.[19][20]

Evaluation has also become more specialized and more safety-aware.[21][22][23][24][25] WorldScore frames world generation as controllability, quality, and dynamics over next-scene tasks.[22] T2VSafetyBench argues that video safety must account for temporal risks such as sequential action and coherent contextual harm, SAFESORA introduces human preference labels for helpfulness and harmlessness, and Video-SafetyBench extends safety evaluation to video LVLMs with a calibrated judge metric.[23][24][25] The common theme is that generic realism scores are no longer sufficient, because models can look plausible yet remain unsafe, unhelpful, or dynamically unstable.[21][22][23][24][25]

## Trends and open problems
Recent work suggests four major trends.[12][15][19][20][22] First, generators are becoming multi-condition systems rather than text-only systems, with image/video references, camera control, and retrieval-augmented motion all serving as useful signals.[17][10][11][15][16][18] Second, the field is moving toward causal or streaming generation to reduce latency and support longer clips, as shown by CausVid and long-horizon training efforts like LongTake.[12][21] Third, audio-video unification is now a first-class goal, not an afterthought.[19][20] Fourth, evaluation is shifting from static quality metrics to benchmarks for dynamics, controllability, safety, and human preference.[22][23][24][25]

The main unresolved problems are long-range temporal degradation, the lack of standardized cross-paper benchmarks, and safety failures that emerge only over sequences rather than single frames.[12][21][23][25] LongTake directly reports that short supervision can make long rollouts static or low quality, which implies that long-horizon dynamics remain hard even for recent autoregressive approaches.[21] T2VSafetyBench and Video-SafetyBench further show that temporal context can create new attack surfaces, so progress in fidelity can coexist with new safety risks.[23][25] Finally, much of the retrieved evidence for 2025–2026 consists of abstracts or project pages rather than full experimental papers, so the current literature still leaves open the need for stronger, standardized evidence on quality, efficiency, and safety tradeoffs.[17][9][11][12][15][18][19][20][22][23][24][25]
